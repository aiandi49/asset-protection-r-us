Article I

Purpose

This model addresses a specific and easily-missed conflict: an outside advisor retained to represent one side of a transaction who secretly holds a beneficial ownership interest in the counterparty — positioning them to profit regardless of which side's interests actually prevail, because their real position was never tied to the outcome they appeared to be working toward.

Article II

What counts as an advisor conflict

An advisor conflict of this kind exists when the person or firm advising you on a transaction holds a direct or indirect beneficial ownership interest in any party on the other side of that transaction — including through layered holding structures specifically designed to obscure the connection from a routine records check. A search of only the immediately visible entity name is not sufficient; the underlying beneficial ownership, however many layers deep, is what actually matters.

In the Northvale case, the advisory firm's controlling ownership ran through a two-layer holding structure to a single individual — the same person serving as the engaging company's outside adviser. The structure had been built roughly eight months before the transaction was even formally proposed.

Article III

The affirmative disclosure requirement

Every outside advisor engagement letter should require written, specific disclosure of any ownership interest, direct or indirect, in any party to a proposed or reasonably anticipated transaction — not a general statement about industry relationships, which discloses nothing specific enough to act on. This disclosure obligation should be continuing, not a one-time signature at engagement, since a conflict can be created after the engagement begins.

  • Disclosure is specific to named parties and named ownership interests, not general relationship descriptions
  • Disclosure is updated at defined intervals throughout the engagement, not only at signing
  • Failure to disclose a conflict that existed but wasn't reported is treated as a material breach of the engagement, with defined consequences stated in the agreement itself

Article IV

Fee timing as a diagnostic

A success fee, by its own definition, compensates an advisor for successfully completed services — and a transaction is not completed until it has been authorized by the appropriate governing body and closed. A success fee that clears before that authorization has occurred is a fact pattern that only makes sense if someone with visibility into the deal had already treated the outcome as decided. Reviewing the exact clearing date of any advisory fee against the exact date of formal board or shareholder authorization should be a standard step in any post-transaction audit, not something checked only once fraud is already suspected.

Article V

Playing both sides: the hedge pattern

The most dangerous version of this conflict isn't simple bias toward one side — it's structural indifference to the outcome. An advisor who has built a position to profit whether the transaction closes on favorable terms, unfavorable terms, or falls apart entirely is not actually working for either side, regardless of who is nominally paying their fee. This kind of arrangement is also frequently paired with self-protective behavior toward whichever party the advisor was ostensibly helping — records, recordings, or leverage kept quietly in reserve — because a person who builds an arrangement like this rarely trusts anyone else involved in it either.

Watch for: an advisor's compensation structure that isn't contingent on the specific outcome they were nominally hired to help achieve · an advisory vehicle formed well before a transaction was proposed, with no other apparent business purpose · an advisor who becomes noticeably more cooperative with investigators once their own exposure to the primary wrongdoing becomes clear.

Article VI

Structural safeguards before engaging any outside advisor

A competitive selection process for significant M&A advisory engagements — even under real time pressure — surfaces conflicts and pricing issues that a single-sourced, relationship-based engagement often doesn't. A basic entity and beneficial-ownership registration search on any advisor's own corporate structure, run before engagement rather than only after suspicion arises, is inexpensive relative to what it protects against. A written conflicts questionnaire, refreshed at defined intervals, keeps the disclosure obligation active rather than treating it as a formality completed once at signing.

Article VII

How this differs from undisclosed seller collateral

The distinction, made explicit: this library's Undisclosed Collateral & Buyer Indemnification Protocol addresses a seller's principal secretly pledging the target company's own assets as collateral for unrelated personal debt — a diligence failure on the assets you're acquiring. This document addresses a different party and a different mechanism entirely: an outside advisor retained to represent one side of a deal who secretly holds a beneficial stake in the counterparty on the other side — a conflict of loyalty, not a diligence gap in the target's balance sheet. The two risks can appear in the same transaction, as they did here, without being the same problem or requiring the same fix.

Article VIII

Limitations & governing law

This model is general guidance and is not a substitute for individualized legal advice. Fiduciary duty standards for outside advisors, and the remedies available for a breach of disclosure obligations, vary by jurisdiction and by the specific engagement agreement. A licensed M&A attorney should draft any real advisor engagement letter, and should be consulted immediately if a real conflict of this kind is suspected.
