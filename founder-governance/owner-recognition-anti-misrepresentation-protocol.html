Article I

Purpose

This model is offered as a plain-language guide for owners and operators who deliberately keep a low public profile — no name on the building, no announced arrivals — and for the staff and counterparties who deal with that ownership structure. It addresses two related failures: an owner being wrongly denied access to their own property, and a third party misusing a personal relationship to fabricate the appearance of insider access for outside investors.

Article II

Confidential ownership registry & verification contact

A small, named list of senior staff — typically the general manager and one or two others — holds a private, verifiable way to confirm an unannounced principal's identity on sight or by a quick call, without requiring the principal to identify themselves publicly or carry proof of ownership on their person.

In the Mariner's Crown incident, this registry existed and worked exactly as intended — Jocelyn Price recognized the owner immediately. The failure was that floor staff and a guest's companion had no reason to know the registry existed at all, and acted on assumptions before anyone with verification authority was in the room.

Article III

No-assumption access policy

Staff at every level are instructed, in writing, that a guest's dress, visible signs of wealth, or a companion's confident assertion are never sufficient grounds to restrict that guest's access or summon security. Any doubt about a guest's standing is resolved by discreetly notifying a member of the verification registry — never by acting on a third party's instruction to remove someone, however senior that third party appears to be.

  • No staff member acts on a demand to remove or restrict a guest issued by another guest or member, regardless of that person's apparent status
  • Doubt about membership or standing is resolved by discreet verification, never by public confrontation
  • Security is summoned only on the instruction of management or the verification registry, never on a bystander's say-so

Article IV

Third-party access & relationship representation clause

Where a counterparty in a business negotiation — a developer, a partner, an investor's representative — implies or states to any third party that they have a personal or professional relationship giving them privileged access to, or influence with, an ownership group, that representation must be affirmatively confirmed in writing by an authorized representative of that ownership group before any party may rely on it. Absent that written confirmation, the representation is treated as unverified and may not be used to solicit investment or favorable terms.

Watch for: a counterparty referencing a personal relationship with someone "connected to" ownership rather than a documented relationship with ownership itself · claims of privileged access that were never confirmed by an authorized representative · investor materials that describe a relationship in terms vague enough to imply more than what was actually granted.

Article V

Compliance referral protocol

A suspected misrepresentation of access or relationship is referred to the compliance function for investigation. The referring principal does not direct the outcome or name a desired result. Once findings are established, they are reported to every party with a legitimate interest in them — including outside investors who may have relied on the misrepresentation — regardless of whether that disclosure is convenient for anyone involved.

Article VI

Consequences of substantiated misrepresentation

Where a misrepresentation of access or relationship is substantiated, pending negotiations with the responsible counterparty are suspended, affected investors and counterparties are notified directly, and the matter is referred for further legal review as warranted. The ownership group does not attempt to manage the responsible party's own institutional consequences — those follow through that party's own governance, separately and without interference.

Article VII

Limitations & governing law

This model is general guidance and is not a substitute for individualized legal advice. Access policies, employment obligations, and disclosure duties to investors vary by state and by the specific agreements in place. A licensed corporate attorney should review any real ownership-verification policy or investor disclosure obligation before it is adopted.

Article VIII

Acknowledgment
