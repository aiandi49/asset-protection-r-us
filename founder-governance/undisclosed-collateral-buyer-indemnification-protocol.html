Key terms, at a glance

The four habits that protect a buyer from inheriting a seller's hidden fraud

Beyond the target's own books

Check what else the seller pledged

Diligence should cover the seller principal's other entities and personal collateral pledges, not just the target company's own financials — fraud often lives in the connections between entities, not inside any one of them.

Representations that bite

Uncapped indemnity for fraud

Purchase agreements should require the seller to affirmatively disclose all collateral pledges and related-party dealings, with an indemnification carve-out for fraud that isn't limited by the deal's general damages cap.

Escalate, don't bury

A written protocol for what you find

When a buyer's own diligence or compliance team flags irregularities after closing, the response is documented escalation to counsel and, where required, to regulators — never a quiet renegotiation that leaves the discovery unaddressed.

Old documents, new lies

Protecting against forged authorization

An old, unsigned proposal or abandoned draft can be misused years later to fabricate a false "authorization" implicating someone who never signed anything. Knowing how to respond quickly matters.

Article I

Purpose

This model addresses a risk that sits outside ordinary purchase-price and closing-mechanics concerns: an acquisition that later turns out to be entangled in the seller principal's undisclosed fraud at a different, related entity — where the buyer's own good-faith diligence becomes the thing that uncovers it, and where the buyer's own historical records can be misused to manufacture false blame.

Article II

Diligence beyond the target's own books

Standard financial diligence reviews the target company's own statements and contracts. It frequently does not extend to whether the seller's controlling principal has pledged the target's assets as collateral for debts held elsewhere, or whether money has moved between the target and other entities the principal controls. A UCC lien search against the seller principal individually, not just the target entity, and a request for a sworn schedule of all related-party transactions and collateral pledges, closes a gap that ordinary diligence often leaves open.

In the Ledger case, the irregularities were visible in the target's transaction records from the start — they were simply misclassified as ordinary accounting discrepancies because no one had reason yet to look for a pattern connecting them to the principal's other companies.

Article III

Representations & warranties that actually bite

The purchase agreement should require the seller to represent affirmatively that no undisclosed collateral has been pledged against the target's assets and that all related-party transactions are listed on an attached schedule. These representations should survive closing for a meaningfully longer period than general representations, and any indemnification obligation tied to a knowing misrepresentation or fraud should carry no damages cap — an ordinary liability cap protects a seller who made an honest mistake, not one who concealed a fraud.

Article IV

The discovery protocol

When a buyer's internal legal, compliance, or finance team flags irregularities that predate the acquisition, the response should follow a defined, documented path rather than an ad hoc judgment call made under pressure.

  • The finding is escalated in writing to outside counsel immediately, before any conversation with the seller or the seller's principal
  • Outside counsel determines whether a regulatory or law enforcement reporting obligation applies, and that determination is documented regardless of outcome
  • No renegotiation, settlement, or informal resolution with the implicated party occurs before counsel has assessed the reporting obligation
  • Internal communications about the finding are treated as privileged and handled accordingly from the first message onward

Article V

Protecting against forged-document exposure

Where an old, unsigned, or abandoned document bearing your name surfaces as part of a fabricated "authorization" you never gave, prompt action matters more than indignation. Retain counsel immediately, request a forensic document examination comparing the disputed document against verified samples of your actual signature and drafting history, and preserve every version of your own historical records — drafts, emails, timestamps — that shows what you actually created and when. Cooperating early and thoroughly with any investigation is what actually clears a name; waiting for the accusation to resolve itself on its own rarely does.

Watch for: an old proposal, term sheet, or draft agreement that was discussed but never executed being resurfaced years later as a "signed" document · a signature that resembles yours but that you have no record of actually placing · an authorization dated during a period when your own records show you were pursuing a different, unrelated deal.

Article VI

Post-discovery governance, if the buyer chooses to help

Where a buyer voluntarily chooses to assist in restructuring a target entangled in a seller's fraud — rather than simply walking away or pursuing indemnification alone — that assistance should come with firm conditions: independent oversight of the restructuring, no operational or governance control retained by the implicated principal, and full, ongoing cooperation with any investigation as a non-negotiable condition of continued assistance. Help offered without these conditions risks becoming complicity in whatever comes next.

Article VII

Limitations & governing law

This model is general guidance and is not a substitute for individualized legal advice. M&A representations and warranties practice, indemnification structures, and fraud reporting obligations vary by jurisdiction and by the specific transaction. A licensed M&A attorney should structure any real acquisition agreement, and a securities or fraud attorney should be engaged immediately if a real discovery of this kind occurs.

Article VIII

Acknowledgment

This guide is intended for general education, inspired by a dramatized story. Any business structuring a real acquisition, or facing a real discovery of undisclosed fraud connected to a deal, should seek the assistance of licensed counsel immediately.
