Article I

Purpose

This model addresses a specific structural safeguard for founder-led or family-built companies: placing the assets that make a business genuinely irreplaceable — not the whole company, just its core — into a trust structure that even the company's own founder cannot unilaterally transfer, sell, or encumber alone. This protects against a threat most governance planning overlooks: not an outside acquirer or a hostile creditor, but an internal actor, including the founder himself in a bad moment, or someone close to him with access and motive.

Article II

What belongs in a crown jewel trust

The trust should hold a narrow, specific set of assets — the things that, if lost, would make the company a fundamentally different and lesser business, not merely a smaller one.

  • Controlling voting shares — a stake large enough to block any board or shareholder action that would fundamentally alter the company, commonly a majority of total voting power
  • Foundational intellectual property the business cannot operate without — the core technology, formula, or method the entire product line depends on
  • The land or facility the operation is genuinely built on, where relocation would be catastrophic rather than merely inconvenient
What does not belong in the trust is everything else — day-to-day operating assets, ordinary equipment, non-critical intellectual property, and anything the business needs to be able to use, sell, or replace in the normal course of operating.

Article III

Funding it before it's worth protecting

The ideal moment to create a trust of this kind is before any outside investment arrives and before the company is large enough for anyone to seriously consider taking it apart. Funding costs and legal complexity are both lowest exactly when a founder is least inclined to think about it — which is precisely why this step gets skipped by companies that later most need it.

Article IV

The constraint that binds the founder too

The defining design choice of this structure is that the primary beneficiary — typically the founder — cannot unilaterally transfer, sell, license, or pledge the trust's assets without independent trustee approval. This is not a limitation imposed by an outside party; it is one the founder or the founder's family deliberately builds in, specifically to survive a future in which the founder's own judgment might be compromised — by a bad year, bad advice, undue influence from someone close to them, or simple human error under pressure.

Article V

Choosing trustees who will actually say no

A trustee's value in this structure is entirely dependent on their willingness to refuse a request from the very person the trust exists to benefit. Longtime family friends, retired professionals with independent standing, or advisors with no financial dependency on the founder's ongoing goodwill tend to serve this role far better than someone who reports to the founder, is compensated by the company, or has personal reasons to avoid conflict with them.

Article VI

What happens when someone tries to sneak trust assets into a deal

Because the trust's assets are documented as belonging to a distinct legal entity with its own approval requirement, any attempt to fold them into a transaction's scope without seeking that approval leaves a clear trail: amended transaction schedules, correspondence expanding a deal's terms, and signature or authorization records that won't show legitimate trustee consent. This trail typically documents the unauthorized attempt far more clearly than the person attempting it realizes at the time, precisely because they are not the one who controls whether the trust's approval requirement gets satisfied.

Watch for: a transaction's scope quietly expanding over time to include assets not part of the original engagement · asset schedules amended without the trust's beneficiary being copied on the changes · an advisor or insider relying entirely on one party's representation that "all necessary authorizations are in place" without independently verifying it.

Article VII

This is not the same as waiting for death

The distinction, made explicit: this library's Business Succession Trust & Interim Control Gap Protocol addresses what happens to controlling ownership in the hours and days after a founder's death. A crown jewel trust is different in kind, not just timing: it operates continuously throughout the founder's life, protecting the company's core from unauthorized transfer at any time, by anyone — including the founder — not only during a succession event. The two structures can and often should coexist in the same company without conflicting.

Article VIII

Limitations & governing law

This model is general guidance and is not a substitute for individualized legal advice. Trust law, corporate governance requirements, and the enforceability of transfer restrictions on voting shares vary significantly by state. A licensed corporate and trusts attorney should structure any real crown jewel trust, ideally at the company's earliest stages.
