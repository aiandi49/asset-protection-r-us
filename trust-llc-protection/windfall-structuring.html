Before Any Strategy
The Distinction That Changes Everything

Protecting money is not the same as un-earning it

With a lottery ticket, the win doesn't legally exist until you claim it — so a trust or LLC can be the one that claims it. Business income through your LLC is different: under what's called the assignment-of-income doctrine, the IRS taxes income to whoever actually earned it, regardless of what entity you route the check through afterward. You can't interpose a new trust or LLC after the deal closes and have the $750M taxed to that entity instead of you — the law looks through the paperwork to who did the work or owned the asset that generated the value.

That means all five strategies below are about what happens to the money after it's taxed and in your hands — protecting it from lawsuits, creditors, opportunistic relationships, and probate exposure — not about avoiding the tax bill itself. The one place timing genuinely changes the tax outcome is if the $750M comes from selling an ownership stake (equity) rather than ordinary business profit, and even then the entity work has to happen years before the sale, not after a deal is on the table. If reducing the tax bill itself is part of the goal, that's a distinct pre-transaction conversation with a tax attorney — ideally 12-24 months before any sale or major contract is signed.

Strategy 01
Single Revocable Trust Claim
revocable-trust-claim.strategy
Simplest
Claim Through a Revocable Living Trust

A trust is formed with a neutral name, and once the $750M lands — as a distribution from your LLC, a sale payout, or a licensing check — it's deposited into and titled in the name of the trust rather than held in your personal name. You remain trustee and retain full control the entire time.

Applied to your LLC year Your LLC still earns the income and still reports it to you (via K-1 if it's a partnership/S-corp, or directly if it's a disregarded single-member LLC) — the trust doesn't change that. What it does is give the after-tax cash a home that isn't your personal name the moment it's distributed out of the business, keeping it off easily searchable personal-asset records like real estate and vehicle titles.
How it works
  • Form a revocable living trust with an attorney, naming a neutral trustee identity — this can happen anytime, even now, well before any payout
  • Retitle your LLC membership interest itself into the trust's name, so future distributions flow to the trust as the member of record
  • The trust holds a dedicated bank account that receives distributions directly from the LLC, rather than routing through your personal checking
  • You continue managing everything as trustee — this is an ownership/paperwork change, not a control change
  • A pour-over will ensures anything that somehow lands outside the trust still funnels into it at death, avoiding probate on those assets
Tax Reality
  • A revocable trust is a "grantor trust" — every dollar is still reported and taxed on your personal Form 1040, exactly as if the trust didn't exist
  • No tax savings, no tax deferral, no change in character (ordinary income stays ordinary, capital gains stay capital gains)
  • On $750M of ordinary business income, combined federal rates (37% top bracket + 3.8% net investment income tax where applicable) put the federal bill in the $280M–$310M range before any state tax — the trust doesn't move that number
Strengths
  • Can be set up today, before any deal even closes
  • Keeps your name off easily searchable personal asset records
  • Zero loss of control — you run it exactly as before
Trade-offs
  • Offers no protection at all from lawsuits, judgments, or creditors — legally it's still your money
  • A judgment creditor can compel you, as trustee, to satisfy a debt from trust assets
Best forThe very first move — set this up now, before the $750M event happens, then layer real protection on top once funds land.
Strategy 02
LLC as Claimant, Trust as Owner
llc-trust-layer.strategy
Two-Layer
A Second, Anonymous Holding LLC Owned by a Trust

Rather than distributions landing directly in your name, a second LLC — formed in a privacy-friendly state — becomes the vehicle that receives and holds the after-tax proceeds. That holding LLC is itself wholly owned by a trust, so two layers sit between your name and any public record.

Applied to your LLC year This is not about your original operating LLC (the one that actually earned the $750M) — that entity's ownership and activity already happened and is what it is for tax purposes. This strategy is about where the money goes after it's distributed out: a fresh holding company, unconnected on paper to your operating business, becomes the thing that owns your investments, real estate, and liquid assets going forward.
How it works
  • Form a new holding LLC in Delaware, Wyoming, or New Mexico — states where member names aren't part of the public formation filing
  • Use a commercial registered agent so your home or office address never appears on the public record
  • Draft an operating agreement naming a trust — not you personally — as the sole member
  • Distributions from your operating LLC flow to you, then are contributed into the holding LLC as capital, where they're invested and managed
  • Open the holding LLC's bank and brokerage accounts under its EIN, with the trust's authorized signers handling day-to-day access
Current federal rule, as of August 2026 The Corporate Transparency Act originally required nearly all LLCs to report their beneficial owners to FinCEN. As of a final rule effective August 14, 2026, U.S. persons and U.S.-formed entities are permanently exempted from that reporting requirement — only foreign entities registering to do business in the U.S. remain in scope. This rule has been through several reversals since 2024, so confirm current status with your attorney before relying on it, but as things stand, a U.S.-formed holding LLC does not need to file beneficial ownership information with the federal government.
Tax Reality
  • Moving after-tax cash into a new LLC is not a taxable event — you're contributing your own already-taxed money as capital
  • If the holding LLC is single-member (owned by your trust) it's a "disregarded entity" by default — its income still flows to the trust's tax return, and since your trust is revocable, ultimately to your personal return
  • No tax reduction here either — this layer is purely about ownership visibility, not tax character
Strengths
  • Two layers between your name and any public-facing ownership record
  • Clean, separate vehicle for future investments, real estate, or new ventures
  • No federal BOI filing burden under the current rule
Trade-offs
  • Banks still require you to disclose beneficial ownership to open accounts, regardless of federal filing rules — this protects you from public searches, not from your bank or the IRS
  • The BOI exemption has reversed direction multiple times since 2024 and could change again
Best forWanting a real paper-trail barrier between your name and your holdings, once the money has already been earned and taxed.
Strategy 03
Irrevocable Trust + Asset-Specific LLCs
irrevocable-segmented.strategy
Protection-Focused
Segmented Holdings Under One Irrevocable Trust

Once the after-tax proceeds are in hand, a portion moves into an irrevocable trust — a real transfer of legal control in exchange for real creditor protection. Underneath that trust sit separate LLCs by asset category: one for real estate, one for a taxable investment portfolio, one for any new business ventures you start with the proceeds.

Applied to your LLC year This is where your original operating LLC — the one that generated the $750M — gets walled off from your new personal wealth. If a customer, employee, or business partner ever sues the operating company, that lawsuit can't reach your investment portfolio or your house if they sit in a separate irrevocable trust structure with no ownership overlap. Keep the operating business's liability entirely separate from where you park your wealth.
How it works
  • An independent trustee — often a corporate/institutional trustee, sometimes a trusted attorney — is named; you cannot serve as your own trustee for this to hold up as irrevocable
  • You irrevocably gift or transfer a portion of the after-tax proceeds into the trust — this uses part of your lifetime gift/estate tax exemption ($15M per individual in 2026) if done as a completed gift, or can be structured as a sale to an intentionally defective grantor trust to avoid using exemption
  • Separate LLCs are formed for real estate, investments, and any new ventures, each owned by the trust
  • Maintain rigorous separation — separate bank accounts, separate books, no commingling — or a court can "pierce the veil" and treat the LLCs as your personal assets anyway
  • Keep your original operating LLC (the one that earned the $750M) entirely outside this structure, so its ongoing liabilities never touch your protected wealth
Tax Reality
  • Funding an irrevocable trust with more than the annual exclusion ($19,000 per recipient in 2026) requires filing a gift tax return and uses part of your $15M lifetime exemption
  • Beyond that exemption, gift tax applies at up to 40% on the amount transferred — so most people fund these gradually or use trust structures designed to avoid immediate gift tax (like a sale to a defective grantor trust) rather than gifting the full amount at once
  • Once irrevocable, income the trust's LLCs generate is typically taxed to the trust itself at compressed trust tax brackets (top rate hits at roughly $15,000 of trust income) unless structured as a grantor trust for tax purposes while still being irrevocable for asset-protection purposes
Strengths
  • Real, court-tested protection from lawsuits and creditors
  • Liability in one asset bucket can't spread into the others
  • Keeps your operating business's risk from ever touching your personal wealth
Trade-offs
  • You give up direct legal control — the independent trustee makes distribution decisions
  • Large transfers trigger gift tax planning and use of lifetime exemption
  • More ongoing accounting and administration across several entities
Best forActually separating your business's liability exposure from your personal wealth — the step most people wish they'd taken before, not after, something goes wrong.
Strategy 04
Domestic Asset Protection Trust
dapt-self-settled.strategy
Specialist Jurisdiction
A DAPT in Nevada, South Dakota, or Delaware

A Domestic Asset Protection Trust is a special irrevocable trust — legal in roughly 19 states — that lets you be both the person who created it and a discretionary beneficiary, unlike ordinary irrevocable trusts. Nevada (NRS Chapter 166) and South Dakota lead here for short "look-back" periods, strong statutory protection, no state income tax on trust earnings, and dynasty-trust durations that can run for centuries.

Applied to your LLC year This is the structure worth considering for the bulk of your $750M after taxes — especially if any part of your business relationship with clients, partners, or investors carries real litigation risk (equity deals, licensing disputes, joint ventures). A DAPT lets you fund it with your own after-tax proceeds while still being allowed to receive distributions from it later, which the plain irrevocable trust in Strategy 03 doesn't allow you to do as cleanly.
How it works
  • Retain an attorney licensed in the chosen DAPT state — your home-state attorney can advise but generally can't draft the trust itself
  • An in-state trustee (often a trust company) is required to administer the trust and hold at least some assets within that state
  • You're named a discretionary beneficiary, so the trustee can make distributions back to you for your benefit, at their discretion
  • Fund it with proceeds well before any dispute, lawsuit, or contract exists against you — Nevada's "look-back" period for challenging a transfer as fraudulent is 2 years, one of the shortest in the country; other states run 4 years
  • Keep meticulous records showing solvency at the time of transfer — the protection can be unwound if you were already insolvent or facing a known claim when you funded it
Tax Reality
  • Most DAPTs are drafted as "grantor trusts" for tax purposes even though they're irrevocable for asset-protection purposes — meaning the income is still taxed on your personal return, which is actually a feature: it lets the trust assets grow tax-free of trust-level tax while you pay the income tax personally, effectively an additional (legal) gift to the trust each year
  • Funding it still counts against your lifetime gift exemption if it's a completed gift beyond the annual exclusion
  • Nevada and South Dakota impose no state income tax on the trust's investment earnings, which matters if you're domiciled in a high-tax state — though your personal income tax residency (not the trust's situs) generally still governs the income taxed to you as grantor
Strengths
  • Statute-backed creditor protection with a short look-back period in Nevada
  • You can still benefit personally as a discretionary beneficiary
  • Favorable state tax treatment on trust investment growth
Trade-offs
  • Requires out-of-state counsel and an in-state trustee relationship
  • Protection is void against a creditor whose claim existed before funding — timing is everything
  • Some states (notably not Nevada or South Dakota) still don't recognize other states' DAPT protections if litigation happens there
Best forThe core of your long-term wealth once liquid, especially if your business dealings carry real litigation exposure — fund it early and let the look-back period run out.
Strategy 05
Private Trust Company + Family Limited Partnership
ptc-flp-structure.strategy
Most Comprehensive
Your Own Trust Company Over a Family Limited Partnership

The most elaborate structure here, typically used above the $100M mark. You charter your own Private Trust Company to serve as trustee across all your trusts, controlled by a board you appoint rather than an outside institution. Underneath, a Family Limited Partnership holds investment and business assets — you or the PTC hold the general-partner control seat, your trusts hold limited-partner economic interests.

Applied to your LLC year At $750M, this is the structure that treats the money as a family enterprise rather than a personal windfall — built to run your operating business, any new ventures you start, and your investment portfolio all under one governance umbrella, and to outlast you. It's overkill if the goal is simply "protect this from a lawsuit," but it's the right scale if the goal is building something that runs for decades and eventually passes to family.
How it works
  • Charter a Private Trust Company, typically in South Dakota, Wyoming, or Nevada, under that state's PTC statute
  • Appoint a board — yourself, an outside attorney, a CPA, and eventually adult children — to govern trustee decisions across all family trusts
  • Form a Family Limited Partnership to hold investment assets and equity in your operating and any future businesses
  • Contribute assets to the FLP in exchange for a general-partner interest (control) and limited-partner interests (economic value), then gift or sell the limited-partner interests into trusts held by the PTC
  • Because limited-partner interests lack control and are hard to sell, they're typically valued at a 20%–35% discount for gift and estate tax purposes — meaning more value can move into trusts per dollar of your $15M lifetime exemption used
Tax Reality
  • The 2026 federal lifetime gift/estate tax exemption is $15M per person ($30M for a married couple), made permanent and inflation-indexed under recent legislation — meaningful at this scale but nowhere near enough to shelter $750M without valuation discounting and years of structured transfers
  • Valuation discounts on FLP interests are real and IRS-recognized, but must be supported by an independent appraisal — aggressive, unsupported discounts are a common audit target
  • A PTC itself doesn't reduce taxes; it's a governance vehicle. The tax efficiency comes from the FLP discounting and from generation-skipping trust techniques layered on top over years, not from the PTC structure itself
Strengths
  • Control stays with people you choose, not an outside institution
  • Combines protection, control, and legitimate gift/estate tax efficiency in one build
  • Built to operate across generations, not just your lifetime
Trade-offs
  • Highest cost and complexity of the five — a standing professional team, not a single attorney
  • Valuation discount strategies draw IRS scrutiny and need defensible appraisals
  • Overkill unless multi-generational planning is genuinely the goal
Best forThinking in decades, not years — long-term family governance, control, and legitimate tax efficiency built into one structure from the start.

The Order That Actually Matters
1
Set up the revocable trust now, before the event
This costs little and takes days — do it regardless of which deeper strategy you pursue later, so the first dollar in already has a better home than your personal name.
2
Bring in a tax attorney before any sale or contract signs
If the $750M involves selling equity rather than ordinary business profit, entity and timing decisions made before signing can matter — after signing, that door is closed.
3
Bring in a high-net-worth CPA for the tax bill itself
At this scale, expect the federal bill alone to land somewhere in the $175M–$310M range depending on income character — plan estimated payments and liquidity for that before spending or transferring anything.
4
Layer in protection once after-tax cash is in hand
This is where Strategies 02 through 05 come in — choose based on how much litigation risk your business actually carries and how long a time horizon you're planning for.
5
Add a wealth manager last
Once the legal and tax structure is settled, investment allocation across the trust and any LLCs can be planned properly — not before.
The Charitable Layer
The Janet Marie Ross Foundation
In loving memory of Janet Marie Ross
Established by The MetaEarth Initiative Trust
janet-marie-ross-foundation.strategy
In Her Name
A Private Foundation, Funded by the Trust

A 501(c)(3) private foundation carrying Janet's name, funded by distributions from the irrevocable trust (Strategy 03 or 04) rather than sitting inside your operating business. Its mission: support for women, children, and animals, and direct help for people who've experienced abuse, homelessness, or hardship. This is a genuine giving vehicle, not a tax-avoidance play — though it does carry the tax treatment described below, since that's simply how the law treats charitable giving.

How the money reaches it The MetaEarth Initiative Trust — not VTREI, LLC, and not the new acquisition holding company below — is the funding source. Keeping the foundation's money separate from every operating business means a lawsuit against one of the companies can never reach Janet's foundation, and the foundation's activities can never create liability for the businesses either. Clean separation both directions.
Forming It
  • Incorporate a nonprofit corporation in your home state, naming it the Janet Marie Ross Foundation, with a board of at least three directors (most states require this, and it can't just be you)
  • Draft bylaws and a mission statement — spell out the four focus areas now (women, children, animals, survivors of abuse/homelessness) so grant-making has a clear mandate from day one
  • File IRS Form 1023 for 501(c)(3) recognition — for a foundation this well-funded, use the full Form 1023, not the short-form 1023-EZ, and expect legal fees in the $10K–$25K range plus several months of IRS review
  • Get an EIN for the foundation, separate from every LLC in the structure
  • Register for charitable solicitation in any state where you'll actively fundraise or promote the foundation beyond your own funding
  • The trust makes its first funding distribution only after 501(c)(3) status is approved, so the gift qualifies for charitable tax treatment from day one
Tax Reality
  • Gifts from the trust to the foundation are tax-deductible for income tax purposes up to 30% of adjusted gross income for cash gifts to a private foundation in a given year, with any excess carried forward up to five years
  • The foundation itself pays a flat 1.39% excise tax on its net investment income (interest, dividends, capital gains) — a genuine cost of doing this as a private foundation rather than through a donor-advised fund
  • Private foundations must distribute roughly 5% of average net investment assets each year in grants or qualifying activities — build that into how the trust funds it, since underfunding one year creates a compliance problem the next
  • Self-dealing rules are strict here: you and your family can serve on the board and set grant direction, but the foundation can't pay you above-market salary, buy assets from you, or lease property from any entity you control — violations carry personal excise tax penalties, not just foundation-level ones
Worth knowing before you file A donor-advised fund at an existing community foundation is a real alternative if the goal is mainly to give effectively without running your own compliance calendar — no excise tax, no minimum distribution requirement, no separate board or Form 1023. You'd lose the standalone name recognition and full control a private foundation gives you, but for many families it's the simpler path to the same charitable outcome. Worth discussing with counsel before committing to the private foundation route.
Best forMaking sure Janet's name carries forward doing exactly what you intend — support for women, children, animals, and people rebuilding after abuse or homelessness — funded cleanly from the trust, walled off from every operating business.

New Entity
A Diversified Acquisition Holding Company
acquisition-holdco.strategy
To Be Formed
A Second LLC, Purpose-Built to Acquire and Diversify

VTREI, LLC is your operating tech company — building it out with acquisitions would mix operating risk with investment risk. A second, separate LLC (working name used below: "MetaEarth Capital, LLC" — pick whatever you actually want to file) owned by the same MetaEarth Initiative Trust gives you a clean, dedicated vehicle for buying real estate, securities, and other businesses with the proceeds, without any of that touching VTREI's balance sheet or liability.

Ten Ways to Deploy It
01
Direct Rental Real Estate
Buy and hold income-producing residential or small multifamily property directly — full control, but hands-on management and concentrated risk in one city's market.
02
Private Real Estate Syndications
Passive investments alongside a sponsor in larger multifamily or commercial deals — real estate exposure across multiple markets without being the landlord.
03
Publicly Traded REITs
Liquid, exchange-traded real estate exposure — the easiest way to diversify property type and geography, tradeable in a brokerage account the holding company controls.
04
A Core Index Fund Portfolio
Broad stock and bond index funds as the steady, low-cost foundation of the holding company — the unglamorous majority allocation that most large family portfolios actually run on.
05
A Municipal Bond Ladder
Federally tax-exempt fixed income, laddered across maturities — a fit for the top tax bracket you're in, providing income without adding to the federal tax bill.
06
Qualified Opportunity Fund Investments
Now permanent under 2025 tax legislation: capital gains reinvested into a Qualified Opportunity Fund get a rolling 5-year deferral, a 10% basis step-up (30% for rural zones), and no tax at all on the new gains if held 10+ years. New zone designations take effect January 1, 2027.
07
Small Business Acquisition (a "Roll-Up")
Buy a handful of small, profitable, already-operating businesses — a search-fund style approach that builds cash-flowing assets faster than starting new ventures from zero.
08

Acquire raw or underdeveloped land in growth corridors around Tucson and Phoenix ahead of infrastructure and population growth — patient capital, low carrying cost, real upside if the area develops.
09

A small, capped allocation (many family offices cap this at 5–10% of the portfolio) into early-stage AI or blockchain companies — letting you stay close to VTREI's world without putting VTREI's own balance sheet at risk.

A "Give-Back" Allocation Sleeve
