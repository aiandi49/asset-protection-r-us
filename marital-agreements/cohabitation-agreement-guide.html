Key terms, at a glance

The four habits that protect two people building a life together without a marriage license

Written, not assumed

No default protections exist

Unmarried partners have none of the automatic property, support, or inheritance rights that marriage creates. Whatever protection either partner wants has to be written down — nothing applies by default.

Separate stays separate

Unless it's put in writing otherwise

Each partner's pre-relationship assets, income, and property remain individually owned. Moving in together doesn't merge finances — only a joint account, joint title, or written agreement does.

Contributions get tracked

Money or labor isn't just absorbed

When one partner puts money, time, or credit into the other's home or business, that contribution is documented at the time — as a loan, an ownership stake, or a defined reimbursement, not an unspoken assumption.

An exit plan, made in advance

Decided before anyone's angry

How shared property, shared debt, and shared living space get divided if the relationship ends is worked out in calm agreement — not negotiated for the first time in the middle of a breakup.

Article I

Purpose

This model is offered as a plain-language guide for two people who share a home, finances, or a life together without being married. Cohabitation is common and the stakes involved — a shared home, years of pooled income, one partner's labor supporting the other's business or household — are often just as significant as marriage, but almost none of the legal protections are automatic. A written agreement is the only way to create them.

Article II

What a cohabitation agreement actually does

A cohabitation agreement is a private contract between unmarried partners covering property, finances, and what happens if the relationship ends. It exists because the law generally does not: unmarried partners typically have no automatic right to share in each other's property, no automatic spousal support obligation, no automatic inheritance without a will, and no automatic authority to make medical decisions for one another. Whatever protections a couple wants have to be created deliberately, in writing, while the relationship is stable.

A small number of states and countries recognize "common law marriage" after a certain number of years together, but the requirements are narrow, inconsistently applied, and not something to rely on without confirming your specific jurisdiction's rule with an attorney.

Article III

Separate property & commingling

Each partner's assets, income, and property owned before the relationship — and anything each acquires individually during it — remain that partner's separate property, unless the couple affirmatively commingles them through a joint account, joint title, or written agreement stating otherwise. Keeping individual accounts alongside a shared account for household expenses preserves clarity about what belongs to whom, without requiring the couple to keep finances entirely apart.

Article IV

Contribution & reimbursement clause

Where one partner contributes money, labor, or credit toward property or a business that legally belongs to the other — paying down a mortgage on a home titled in one partner's name, or working unpaid in the other's business — that contribution should be documented in writing at the time it happens. The agreement should state whether the contribution is a gift, a loan to be repaid, or converts into a defined ownership percentage. Absent that documentation, the contributing partner typically has no legal claim to anything in return, regardless of how many years or how much money was involved.

  • Contributions toward a home titled solely in one partner's name are tracked with dated records — receipts, bank transfers, a signed acknowledgment
  • Labor contributed to a partner's business is valued and documented, even informally, at the time it's given
  • The agreement states plainly whether tracked contributions are gifts, loans, or equity — silence defaults to nothing owed

Article V

Shared property & debt

Property purchased jointly — most commonly a shared home — is divided on separation according to a formula agreed to in advance, typically tied to each partner's documented financial contribution rather than an assumed equal split. Unmarried partners are not automatically entitled to an equal division the way divorcing spouses often are in community-property states; without a written formula, division of jointly titled property depends on who is listed on the title and deed, regardless of who actually paid for it.

Article VI

What happens if it ends

The agreement lays out, in advance, the practical mechanics of separating: a timeline for dividing shared property, a process for one partner to buy out the other's interest in a shared home, how joint accounts and joint leases are unwound, and who remains in shared housing during a transition period. Deciding this while the relationship is stable avoids the far more common alternative — negotiating it for the first time during the breakup itself, under the worst possible conditions for a fair outcome.

Article VII

No automatic rights — a reminder

Beyond property, unmarried partners typically have no automatic right to spousal support, no automatic inheritance from one another without a will naming them, and no automatic authority to make medical decisions if a partner is incapacitated. Couples who want these protections need companion documents alongside the cohabitation agreement itself.

Consider alongside this agreement: a will naming your partner as a beneficiary, if that's your intent · a durable power of attorney and healthcare proxy naming your partner · beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and life insurance updated to reflect your actual wishes · a review of what happens to a shared home's title if one partner dies, since jointly titled property with rights of survivorship behaves differently than property left through a will.

Article VIII

Limitations & governing law

This model is general guidance and is not a substitute for individualized legal advice. The enforceability of cohabitation agreements, and the rights of unmarried partners generally, vary significantly by state and country — some jurisdictions enforce these agreements much like any other contract, while others impose additional requirements. A licensed family law attorney should draft any real cohabitation agreement in the couple's actual jurisdiction.
