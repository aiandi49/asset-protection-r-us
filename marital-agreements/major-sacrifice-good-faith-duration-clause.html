Key terms, at a glance

The four habits that protect a spouse who gives something that can never be given back

Named and dated

A sacrifice, defined in writing

Major uncompensated sacrifices — organ donation, funding a spouse's treatment, a career sacrificed for the other's earning years — are named and time-stamped when they happen, not reconstructed from memory during a divorce.

A price, not a cage

You can't be forced to stay married

No clause can require someone to remain in a marriage. What it can do is define what's owed if the receiving spouse leaves shortly after receiving a sacrifice of this kind — pricing the exit rather than blocking it.

Courts already watch for this

Bad-faith timing moves judges

Even without a written clause, evidence that a spouse was already planning to leave while accepting a major sacrifice can shift how a court divides assets. A clause makes the claim clear instead of hoping a judge finds equivalent facts.

Written down as it happens

Contemporaneous records win cases

Dated medical records, a signed acknowledgment at the time of the sacrifice, and honesty in writing during major life events are what actually persuade a court — not recollection of good faith after the fact.

Article I

Purpose

This model addresses a gap that ordinary prenuptial and postnuptial agreements rarely cover: what happens when one spouse makes an extraordinary, irreversible, and uncompensated sacrifice for the other — most starkly an organ or tissue donation, but also funding a spouse's medical treatment or education, or giving up years of earning potential to support the other's career — and the receiving spouse ends the marriage shortly afterward, in circumstances suggesting the decision to leave predated the sacrifice itself.

Article II

What counts as a major uncompensated sacrifice

To be enforceable and to avoid becoming a vague, contestable claim, a "major sacrifice" should be defined narrowly and specifically rather than left open-ended:

  • Organ, tissue, or bone marrow donation to the other spouse
  • Direct funding of a spouse's medical treatment, surgery, or long-term care above a defined dollar threshold
  • A career pause or exit of a defined minimum duration undertaken primarily to support the other spouse's career, business, or education
  • Primary, sustained caregiving through a spouse's serious illness or disability, documented by a treating physician
Narrow, specific categories are what make this enforceable. A clause covering "any significant sacrifice" invites exactly the kind of dispute it's meant to avoid.

Article III

The good-faith duration provision

The clause does not prevent either spouse from filing for divorce at any time — no agreement legally can. What it does is establish that if the receiving spouse initiates divorce within a defined window after a qualifying sacrifice — commonly 18 to 24 months — a rebuttable presumption arises that the receiving spouse owes the sacrificing spouse a defined equitable adjustment: a share of earnings during a following period, reimbursement of related costs, or a stated sum agreed in advance. The presumption is rebuttable — a receiving spouse can present evidence the timing was coincidental and unrelated to the sacrifice — but the burden shifts to them to explain it, rather than requiring the sacrificing spouse to prove bad faith from nothing.

Article IV

Why courts already have this power, with or without a clause

Most jurisdictions give family courts real discretion to weigh conduct and bad faith in dividing marital assets, even without a pre-existing agreement. A documented pattern — messages showing a spouse was planning departure while accepting the other's sacrifice, filing papers within days of a major surgery, timing that strains coincidence — is exactly the kind of evidence that can move a court's equitable distribution ruling substantially, as it did without any written clause at all in the case that inspired this model. A written provision doesn't replace this judicial discretion; it removes the uncertainty of hoping a judge happens to find equivalent facts and gives both spouses clarity going in.

Article V

Contemporaneous documentation

The single greatest determinant of whether a claim like this succeeds is documentation created at the time, not reconstructed afterward. Medical records establishing the date and nature of the sacrifice, a brief written acknowledgment signed by both spouses near the time it occurs, and ordinary caution in written communication during major medical or financial life events all matter — because messages sent casually in the moment often become the evidence a court relies on later, for better or worse.

Article VI

A related, separate risk worth naming

Financial authority granted to a partner during a period of vulnerability — a power of attorney given "for convenience" while recovering, property retitled into a partner's name "for tax reasons," or account authorizations signed without close review — is a distinct vulnerability from the one this clause addresses, and deserves its own dedicated safeguards rather than being folded into a marital sacrifice clause. That is a strong candidate for a companion guide in its own right.

Article VII

Limitations & governing law

This model is general guidance and is not a substitute for individualized legal advice. The willingness of courts to weigh bad-faith conduct or timing in property division varies enormously by state — some jurisdictions are strictly no-fault with limited room for this kind of factor, while others give judges broad equitable discretion. A licensed family law attorney should draft any real version of this clause in the couple's actual jurisdiction, and confirm it would be enforced as intended there.
