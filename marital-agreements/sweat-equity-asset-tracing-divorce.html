Article I

Purpose

This model addresses two connected problems that surface constantly in long marriages built around one spouse's formally titled business: how uncompensated, undocumented labor and capital contributed to that business gets recognized — or erased — at divorce, and how money that originated during the marriage can be moved through new entities in an attempt to recharacterize it as separate property immediately before a filing.

Article II

Documenting an uncompensated contribution as it happens

The single most important habit, and the hardest one to start after the fact, is keeping a contemporaneous record of substantive contributions to a spouse's business — not for suspicion, but because memory alone rarely survives a deposition years later. A dated note describing a problem found and fixed, a saved draft of a financial model with a timestamp, an email or text referencing the work as it happened, all carry weight that recollection does not.

  • Save dated drafts of any financial models, projections, or structuring work, even informal ones
  • Keep a simple, ongoing log of significant contributions — a caught contract clause, a restructured loan package, a weekend spent rebuilding a submission — noted at the time, not reconstructed later
  • Retain records of any personal capital contributed to a spouse's business, including the source of that capital and the date it was contributed
In the Bracken Hill case, a single sentence — "he helped me with some spreadsheets" — nearly erased nine years of deal-saving work in a deposition. What prevented it from succeeding was a documentary record that existed independently of anyone's memory or goodwill.

Article III

What forensic tracing actually does

A forensic accountant engaged in a high-asset divorce traces the origin of disputed funds through a full chain of transactions — following money through however many intermediary entities it passes, comparing entity formation and restructuring dates against the timeline of the relationship, and reconstructing where value actually originated regardless of which name currently sits on the account. This is methodical, often slow work, and it is what actually resolves disputes that would otherwise come down to competing verbal accounts.

Article IV

Why moving money through a new entity doesn't erase its character

Property generally keeps its marital or separate character based on how and when it was actually acquired, not based on which entity happens to hold title to it today. A newly formed LLC receiving the proceeds from the sale of marital-era assets does not automatically convert those proceeds into that entity owner's separate property, however cleanly the new entity's paperwork is written. Courts and forensic accountants look through the entity to the underlying origin of the funds.

Article V

Timing as a red flag

An entity's formation, an ownership restructuring, or a rewritten statement of purpose occurring in the weeks or months immediately before a divorce filing is a fact pattern worth documenting precisely, because the timing itself becomes part of the evidence. Note exact dates of any structural changes to shared or business assets alongside the timeline of the relationship's breakdown — the proximity, once laid out plainly, often speaks for itself.

Watch for: a new entity formed, or an existing one restructured, in the months before a major relationship change · ownership documents amended to newly describe an asset as one spouse's separate property · a sudden reluctance to discuss "a difficult negotiation" that coincides with unexplained absences or distraction.

Article VI

Reading a settlement offer's size against what it's asking for

A settlement offer that is unusually generous specifically in exchange for ending a financial review or discovery request — rather than reflecting a reasoned valuation of the actual disputed assets — is itself informative. An offer sized to make scrutiny stop, rather than to fairly value what is being discussed, is not a gesture of goodwill; it is a data point about how the offering party assesses their own position if that scrutiny continues.

Article VII

What mediation can resolve that continued litigation often can't

Once a strong evidentiary record exists — a documented contribution history, a completed forensic tracing report — a skilled mediator familiar with that full record can often reach a fair division reflecting the actual facts faster and at lower cost than continuing to litigate every disputed dollar. The value of mediation depends entirely on the strength of the record brought into it; mediating before the tracing work is complete usually just relocates the same unresolved dispute into a different room.

Article VIII

Limitations & governing law

This model is general guidance and is not a substitute for individualized legal advice. The characterization of marital versus separate property, the treatment of commingled or transmuted assets, and the recognition of uncompensated contributions to a spouse's business all vary significantly by state. A licensed family law attorney and a qualified forensic accountant should be engaged directly in any real dispute of this kind.
