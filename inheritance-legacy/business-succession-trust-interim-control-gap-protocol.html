Key terms, at a glance

The four habits that kept a founder's company from being seized in the hours after his death

Outside the will

Controlling shares live in a trust, not the estate

A trust holding voting control is a separate legal structure from the will — it isn't frozen, contested, or delayed by probate, and it keeps operating on its own terms from the moment it was created.

A deadline, not a formality

A defined window to register standing

The named trustee has a strict, short deadline — commonly 72 to 96 hours from when the estate opens — to formally register and present standing to the board. Missing it opens a real door.

Know the gap exists

Plan around the vulnerability in advance

Anyone positioned to inherit control of a closely-held company should know this exact window exists before the day they're actually in it — not learn about it while the clock is already running.

Down to the minute

Timestamps decide who prevails

When two parties compete for control during succession, the sequence of official filings and court orders — not who acted first informally, not who has more support in the room — is what actually resolves it.

Article I

Purpose

This model addresses a specific, often-overlooked vulnerability in business succession planning: the gap between a founder's death and the formal, board-recognized establishment of whoever inherits controlling ownership. That gap can be measured in hours, and in a closely-held company, hours are enough time for someone already positioned inside the company to attempt to fill it.

Article II

Why controlling shares belong in a trust, not the will

A will becomes part of the public probate estate — subject to court filing timelines, potential contests, and delay that can run into months even in uncomplicated cases. A properly funded trust holding a company's controlling shares operates independently of that process, on its own governing terms, without needing probate court approval to keep functioning. Structured this way, control can transfer immediately upon death rather than waiting for an estate to formally settle.

In the Keystone case, the trust was never named in the will at all — because it was never part of the estate. It existed alongside it, on its own terms, from the day it was funded years earlier.

Article III

The defined standing window

A well-drafted succession trust includes a governing clause requiring the named trustee to formally register with the appropriate authority and present documentation to the company's board within a fixed period after the estate opens. This deadline exists deliberately, not as an oversight to fix later: it forces decisive action from the named successor and prevents a passive heir, however legitimately named, from leaving a dangerous vacuum at the exact moment the company can least afford one.

Article IV

The interim steward risk

If the standing window closes without the named trustee completing registration, most governing structures allow an interim steward to be appointed to administer the shares until standing is established. This exists as a safety valve for genuine incapacity or delay — not as an invitation. But anyone aware that a controlling trust exists at all, even without knowing who was actually named trustee, can attempt to file for interim status the moment the window opens. The only real defense is completing registration as early and as fast as legally possible, not waiting for a comfortable moment that may not come.

Watch for: anyone moving to fill a "vacancy" in company control within hours of a founder's death, before a will has even been formally read · a petition for interim or temporary authority filed suspiciously close to the moment an estate opens · confidence from a party about controlling a trust's votes without ever having confirmed who the actual named trustee is.

Article V

What to do the moment you learn you're the trustee

Contact the attorney who drafted the trust immediately, before doing anything else. Submit registration filings the same day if at all possible, and ask explicitly whether any expedited or same-day processing option exists. Notify the company's board formally and promptly — even before full confirmation clears — so the board has the information on record regardless of how quickly the paperwork itself moves. Document the exact time of every filing, call, and submission along the way.

Article VI

Timestamps as the deciding factor

Courts and boards resolve competing claims to control during a succession dispute based on official timestamps — the minute a filing was received, the minute a court order was signed, the minute a board resolution passed — not on who arrived first informally or who had more allies in the room that day. A trustee registration confirmed before a competing provisional order is signed generally supersedes that order once formally established. Keep meticulous, contemporaneous records of exactly when each step happened and who can verify it.

Article VII

Choosing the right people before you ever need them

A succession plan is only as fast as the people who have to execute it under pressure. A drafting attorney who moves quickly and is genuinely reachable in a crisis, and at least one trusted insider — a CFO or comparable executive — willing to act on legitimate concerns rather than defer to whoever currently holds operational power, are often what actually makes the difference between a plan that works and one that exists only on paper.

Article VIII

This is not the same as a crown jewel trust

The distinction, made explicit: this document addresses what happens to controlling ownership in the hours and days immediately after a founder's death — a time-boxed, death-triggered gap. This library's Crown Jewel Asset Trust addresses a different problem entirely: protecting a company's core assets from unauthorized transfer at any time during a founder's life, by anyone — including the founder — not only during a succession event. The two structures are complementary and often belong in the same company at once, not substitutes for each other.

Article IX

Limitations & governing law

This model is general guidance and is not a substitute for individualized legal advice. Trust law, succession statutes, and the enforceability of a defined standing window vary significantly by state. A licensed trusts and estates attorney should structure any real succession trust of this kind, well before it is ever needed.
